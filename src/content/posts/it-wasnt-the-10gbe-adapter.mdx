If you do networking for a living, you already know what's wrong here and the
second code block will bore you. I don't, and it cost me an evening, a wrong
hypothesis, and very nearly the price of a replacement adapter. So this is the
whole path rather than the answer, including the part where I confirmed a
diagnosis I hadn't actually confirmed.

I bought a Thunderbolt 10G adapter to get a Mac mini talking to a UNAS Pro at
something better than gigabit. I got gigabit.

Everything I knew about these adapters pointed at the adapter. Thermal
throttling, driver problems, chipset lottery. It was the newest and least
trusted thing in the path and I was ready to send it back.

It was fine. The Mac and the NAS were on different subnets, so every packet
between two machines hanging off the same switch was going up to the gateway to
be routed and coming back down again. They were on different subnets because of
a filing decision I'd made years earlier, for a reason that sounds principled
and isn't.

## What I actually measured, which is less than I'd like

Here is my entire before-measurement: I said "I'm seeing gigabit speeds."

That's it. No throughput figure, no read/write split, no note on whether the
number sat flat or decayed over the length of a transfer. I know it was in the
gigabit neighbourhood because that's what I said at the time, and I can't tell
you anything more precise than that, because I didn't write it down.

That matters more than it looks, because a flat ceiling and a decaying one point
at different classes of problem, and I threw the distinction away before I
started.

The one thing I did check was the link itself:

```
❯ ifconfig en18
en18: flags=8863<UP,BROADCAST,SMART,RUNNING,SIMPLEX,MULTICAST> mtu 1500
	options=567<RXCSUM,TXCSUM,VLAN_MTU,TSO4,TSO6,AV,CHANNEL_IO>
	inet 192.0.2.10 netmask 0xffffff00 broadcast 192.0.2.255
	media: autoselect (10Gbase-T <full-duplex>)
	status: active
```

`10Gbase-T`, full duplex, active. The physical layer had finished negotiating
and it had negotiated what I paid for. Anything slow after that point is
happening above the link, which is the useful thing this check buys you: it
splits "the hardware can't" from "the path won't."

The switch end I only checked after the fact: the Mac is on one of the switch's
10G ports. What I never did was look at the speed the controller reported that
port negotiating, at the time or since — which is the check I'd tell you to run
and can't claim I ran.

## The evidence turned up while I was testing the wrong theory

The leading theory wasn't the dongle by this point. It was that the traffic
wasn't on the dongle at all. The mini has a built-in 1G port, and if that was
also plugged in, macOS could plausibly have been sending storage traffic out the
slow one. So I asked which interface the traffic actually used:

```
route get 198.51.100.10
   route to: nas.example.com
destination: nas.example.com
    gateway: router.example.com
  interface: en18
      flags: <UP,GATEWAY,HOST,DONE,WASCLONED,IFSCOPE,IFREF,GLOBAL>
 recvpipe  sendpipe  ssthresh  rtt,msec    rttvar  hopcount      mtu     expire
       0         0         0         6         8         0      1500         0
```

`interface: en18` answered the question I asked. The traffic was on the 10G
adapter, and the theory I'd run the command for was dead.

The answer to the question I hadn't asked is two lines above it. `gateway:` is
populated with the router, and `flags:` contains `GATEWAY`. The destination
isn't on-link. Read that against the `inet` line from `ifconfig` and the two
addresses sit in different `/24`s.

So the path was never Mac to switch to NAS. It was Mac, switch, gateway, switch,
NAS, for every packet.

Two things I'd do differently: run the diagnostic even when you expect it to
exonerate your suspect, and read the whole output instead of the field you asked
for. The line that mattered was on screen the first time I looked at it.

## What that proves, and what it doesn't

It proves the path is routed. It does not prove the routed path is what was slow.

I skipped straight past that distinction — concluded the gateway was the
bottleneck, then designed the test that would show it. Wrong order, and it set
up the mistake two sections down.

I still can't tell you how fast that gateway forwards between subnets, because I
never measured it, and I never checked whether IDS/IPS was enabled either. Deep
packet inspection on the forwarding path is the usual explanation for a gateway
capping well below its rated throughput, so I can't separate a forwarding limit
from an inspection tax. "The router was in the path, and it shouldn't have been"
is the part I have evidence for.

## Why the NAS was somewhere stupid

When I set the network up, I had the idea that it's a UniFi device, so all UniFi
hardware should go on the management network. That doesn't really hold up.

A management network is for managing infrastructure: switch consoles, access
point control planes. The NAS is a server that happens to be made by the same
vendor as the switch, and its data plane has no more business on the control
plane than the Mac's does.

The filing decision came with a trap as well. UniFi can hand a network's
inter-VLAN routing to an L3-capable switch instead of the gateway, which is the
architecturally correct fix here; Ubiquiti's
[Layer 3 Routing](https://help.ui.com/hc/en-us/articles/360042281174-Layer-3-Routing)
article describes it as "setting the Router option on a new or existing Virtual
Network to the UniFi Switch."

Note the words *Virtual Network*. When I looked, the Default network gave me no
way to select the switch. I can't tell you that's a documented restriction,
because I went and checked: the Default network isn't mentioned in that article
at all, and nothing on Ubiquiti's help site says it's excluded — or that it's
included. So take it as something I hit on my own controller rather than a rule.
If your storage lives on the Default network, confirm you can delegate its
routing before you plan a migration that assumes you can.

There's a documented cost if you can: "Traffic Identification and features that
rely on it are not supported on networks managed by an L3 switch." You buy
hardware forwarding and give up the gateway's view of what that traffic is.

## The confirmation, and the bar it didn't clear

Before touching any of the architecture, the cheap test is to put both machines
on the same network temporarily and re-run the benchmark. I wrote the threshold
down before running it: 900+ MB/s would confirm the gateway was the bottleneck.

Same network, Blackmagic Disk Speed Test, 5 GB test file:

- writes 200–300 MB/s
- reads 300–700 MB/s, fluctuating

That is not 900 MB/s. It is also not gigabit, and every number in it clears the
old ceiling comfortably, so routing through the gateway was a real bottleneck and
moving the traffic off it was a real improvement.

But I called it a confirmation at the time, and it isn't a clean one. I set a
bar, missed it, and accepted the result anyway by quietly promoting the 700 MB/s
peak to "the result." The floor of that range is 300. Writing the threshold down
beforehand isn't rigour for its own sake — the number on the page is the only
thing that makes moving it visible afterwards.

The shortfall wasn't the network either. The obvious suspect for a fluctuating
read range was the NAS's NVMe cache — two 2 TB drives, vastly more than a 5 GB
test file — but the appliance's own cache panel ruled that out: 16% read hit
rate, 7% write hit rate, and the cache 90% full of a working set unrelated to the
benchmark. If most reads were coming off the spinning array instead, 300 MB/s is
roughly array speed and the 700 MB/s bursts are the occasional cache hit. I'll
leave the write path alone, because a 7% write hit rate and "the writes were
absorbed by cache" can't both be true and I don't know which one is wrong.

## Where this actually ended

The proper fix — NAS onto its own storage network, inter-VLAN routing handed to
the switch — is not done. I have a plan and no deployment, and the same-subnet
test isn't a fix. It's a test I've left in place.

What did ship was the thing that makes the move safe later, and it only exists
because the first version of the plan put it in the wrong place. It was the last
step, filed as a nice-to-have: once everything's moved, switch from hardcoded
addresses to hostnames so the next change is easier.

Last is the wrong place for it. The cutover then changes the NAS's address and
every consumer's configuration simultaneously, so anything that breaks
afterwards is ambiguous between a routing fault and a stale mount.

Front-loading it removes the ambiguity. Point every consumer at
`nas.example.com` while the address is still the old one and nothing has moved.
Verify each one resolves by name, then power-cycle it and verify again, because a
mount that works until something restarts three weeks later isn't done. Only
then move the box: one DNS record changes, and if something breaks at that point
it broke because it wasn't going through DNS like you thought it was.

That's the piece I'd keep if I could only keep one, and it has nothing to do with
10GbE.

## Takeaways

1. **Run `route get <destination>` before you suspect hardware.** If `gateway:`
   is populated and `flags:` says `GATEWAY`, your two machines aren't talking
   over the switch, whatever the switch cost. It costs ten seconds and it
   eliminates the expensive wrong answer, which is returning a working adapter.
2. **Read the whole diagnostic, not the field you asked for.** Mine killed the
   theory I ran it for in the line I was watching, and named the real one two
   lines above.
3. **Write the success threshold down before you run the test.** Otherwise you
   will find yourself confirming a diagnosis against the best number in a
   fluctuating range, and it will feel like rigour at the time.
4. **Check the network can actually delegate its routing before planning around
   it.** Ubiquiti documents the feature; it doesn't document which networks
   don't offer it, and on my controller the Default network didn't.
5. **"All the vendor's devices belong on the management network" is not a design
   principle.** It's a filing habit, and it put a storage array on a control
   plane where it quietly cost me most of a 10G link.
