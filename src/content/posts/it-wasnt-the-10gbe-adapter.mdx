If you buy a 10GbE adapter, plug it into a 10GbE switch port, and copy a file to
a NAS with a 10GbE uplink, you expect something in the neighborhood of 10 Gbps.
I was getting about 2.4.

Everything I read pointed at the dongle. Thunderbolt 10G adapters have a
reputation — thermal throttling, driver problems, chipset lottery — and mine was
the newest, least-trusted thing in the path. I was close to sending it back.

The adapter was fine. My traffic was leaving the switch, going up to the router,
getting forwarded between two subnets in software, and coming back down. The
ceiling I kept hitting wasn't the link. It was the router's forwarding path.

produces a sawtooth — good throughput for a while, then a fall off a cliff as
it heats up, then partial recovery. A hard, flat ceiling that shows up on the
first second of every transfer and never moves is a different kind of problem.
It says something in the path has a fixed capacity, and you're sitting on it.

protocol on top.

## The thing I wasn't looking at

The Mac and the NAS were on different subnets.

That single fact was invisible to me for a long time, because at the application
layer nothing about it looks like a problem. The share mounts. Names resolve.
Transfers work. Nothing errors out. It just goes slower than it should, forever.

Traffic between two hosts on the same subnet gets switched — it stays on the
switching fabric, which is built to move traffic at line rate and does it in
hardware. Traffic between two subnets has to be *routed*, and on this network
the thing doing the routing was the UDM Pro. Once a packet has to go up to the
router and back to get to a machine on the same physical switch, the throughput
you get is whatever the router's forwarding path can do — not what the link can
do.

That's the whole bug. It's not a misconfiguration in the sense that anything was
set wrong; it's a topology decision, made for perfectly reasonable
segmentation reasons, quietly setting a bandwidth ceiling on a path I expected
to be fast.

## The fix

Same-subnet placement. Put the Mac and the NAS on the same network so the
traffic between them is switched rather than routed, and the router stops being
in the path at all.

## Why this is worth writing down

The failure mode here is that the wrong hypothesis is *expensive*. A 10GbE
adapter that seems to underperform gets returned. The replacement behaves
identically, because the replacement was never the problem, and now you've spent
two weeks and a shipping label to learn nothing.

The tell that would have saved me time: ask whether the two machines are on the
same subnet *before* you suspect any hardware. It costs ten seconds and it
eliminates the most likely non-obvious cause.

## Takeaways

1. **A flat ceiling is a capacity limit; a sawtooth is a thermal problem.** The
   shape of the graph tells you which class of problem you have before you know
   anything else.
2. **Confirm the negotiated link speed early.** It cleanly separates "the
   hardware can't" from "the path won't."
3. **Same-subnet traffic is switched; cross-subnet traffic is routed.** On a
   home network the router is almost never as fast as the switch, so any path
   that crosses subnets inherits the router's ceiling.
4. **Segmentation has a throughput cost, and nothing tells you.** No error, no
   warning — just a number that's lower than it should be, indefinitely.
5. **Suspect the topology before the newest component.** The new part is the
   obvious suspect, which is exactly why it deserves less benefit of the doubt
   than your instinct gives it.
